# Red-Team Critique — Wabi-Sabi Menu Review System
## Implementation Plan v1.0 (2026-06-15)

**Reviewer stance:** skeptical principal product designer + research-integrity reviewer. This is an unusually thoughtful plan — it *names* more bias vectors than most production systems even consider. That sophistication is also why it deserves a harder pass: a plan this articulate about integrity risks should not leave as many of them only *named* rather than *closed*. The findings below assume the plan is flawed and look for where.

---

## Scores (1–10)

| Lens | Score | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Bias & integrity** | **6/10** | Best-in-class *taxonomy* of risks; several mitigations are either cosmetic, self-undermining, or simply don't survive the real menu/floor-plan context. |
| **2. Diner UX / friction** | **4/10** | The core promise ("<90s, zero friction") collides head-on with the locked "no POS, full 183-item menu" decision. As specified, the realistic median flow is 2–4x the target. |
| **3. Admin usefulness** | **5/10** | Good instincts (Δ-framing, top/bottom lists) undermined by a statistics problem the plan doesn't address: most of 183 dishes will have N=0–3/week, and the dashboard presents noise as signal with no uncertainty framing. |

Several concrete specs (focus-ring contrast, label-driven layout shift, AI-texture risk, an actual ⚠️ emoji in the admin wireframe) would visibly betray that system on day one. |

## LENS 1 — Bias & Integrity

### CRITICAL

**1.1 — "Already reviewed" + 12-hour service window doesn't model the dominant real scenario: shared/borrowed phones at a table.**
The anonymity/anti-double-review model is built entirely around *one phone = one diner = one session cookie*. But the most common real pattern at a table of 2–6 is: **one person scans the QR, the phone gets passed around** ("here, rate the salmon, I'll rate the curry"), or **multiple people scan the same physical QR code independently** on their own phones. The plan's model handles the second case correctly (each phone = its own session, fine). It does *not* address the first case — and the first case is where the "one-review-per-dish-per-session" constraint actively produces **worse data**: if Person A starts the flow and Person B takes over the phone to add their own per-dish opinions, the system records this as **one coherent diner's voice**, when it's actually 2+ people's opinions merged into one session — *increasing* the appearance of consensus on a 1–5 scale that's supposed to represent one person's experience.
*Why it matters:* This directly inflates "engagement quality" (Section 1.2's variance metric) in the wrong direction — a session with high internal variance might just be reflecting *honest disagreement between two diners*, not one diner's nuanced take, and the admin has no way to distinguish these.
*Fix:* Either (a) explicitly design for "rate together" — e.g., the landing screen asks "How many of you are reviewing? [1] [2+]" purely as a *framing* device (no extra friction, no extra DB columns needed, just sets diner expectations and could tag `review_sessions.group_size_hint` for the admin's "engagement quality" interpretation), or (b) explicitly accept and *document* this as a known limitation in the admin-facing copy on `/admin/integrity` ("ratings reflect whoever held the phone — may represent more than one diner's view"). Right now it's neither designed for nor disclosed.

**1.2 — "Excluded from headline averages by default" (is_low_effort) is a curation lever wearing a statistics costume.**
Section 3.5 makes a big, correct point: *no UI control should let the admin discard data, because that's how curation happens.* But Section 3.4 #5 does exactly that at the system level: reviews under ~5 seconds are **automatically excluded from headline averages by default**, with "a toggle to include them." This is a *default-on filter that changes the number David sees* based on a heuristic (`time_spent_seconds < 5`) that has **zero validation** in this plan against the actual diner population. A diner who already has strong opinions (loved the salmon, hated the soup — decided before they even sat down to review) can legitimately blow through a 2-question flow in 4 seconds. The plan's own Section 3.4 #5 *acknowledges* this ("never silently dropped — that would itself be a bias vector if a real human is just fast") and then... silently drops it from the default view anyway. The "toggle to include" framing doesn't fix this: **defaults are policy**. Whoever sets the 5-second threshold (an engineer, in Phase 1, with no real data) is making an editorial decision about whose voice counts in the number David sees by default.
*Why it matters:* This is the single mechanism most likely to be *unconsciously* tuned over time in the direction of "fewer scary numbers" — not through malice, but through "the 5s threshold felt too aggressive/lax, let's adjust it" conversations that have no audit trail (unlike `moderation_log`, there's no log of threshold changes).
*Fix:* Either (a) don't exclude by default — show the *raw* number as the headline and surface "N reviews flagged as fast — view breakdown" as a secondary annotation (inverting the default), or (b) if exclusion-by-default ships, log every threshold change to an append-only table exactly like `moderation_log`, and surface "headline average excludes 3 fast reviews (toggle to see all)" *on the dashboard itself*, not buried in `/admin/integrity`.

**1.3 — Anonymity is architecturally real but *perceptually* fragile — and perception is what drives honesty (the plan's own stated goal).**
RLS-level anonymity (3.1, 3.2, 9.2) is genuinely well-engineered — the anon key truly cannot read review data back. But a diner doesn't know or care about RLS. What they *perceive* is: **a staff member places a physical card with a QR code on their specific table**, the table number is visibly printed on that card, and the diner scans it *while staff are still present, clearing plates, hovering nearby*. The URL itself (`/r/t12`) and the landing copy ("Table 12") **visibly identifies the table to the diner**, even if the backend treats it as "where, not who." For a 2-top on a quiet Tuesday, "Table 12" *is* "the couple by the window that the server just talked to for 45 minutes" — from the diner's seat, this doesn't feel anonymous, it feels **traceable to a specific, recent, identifiable interaction**, regardless of what the database stores.
*Why it matters:* This is the single biggest threat to "honest, unbiased" (G3) that the plan doesn't engineer for at all — it's a *perception* problem, and the plan only solves the *architecture* problem. Social-desirability bias doesn't care about your RLS policies.
*Fix:* This needs explicit copy + timing design: (a) the landing screen copy should *say* something reassuring and specific — not generic "your feedback is anonymous" boilerplate (which itself can read as defensive/corporate, violating brand voice) but something that addresses the *table-identifiability* concern directly and warmly, e.g., framing around "shared across all tables tonight" rather than "your table's feedback"; (b) consider whether the table code should even be *visible* in the URL/UI to the diner at all post-scan (it's needed for rate-limiting server-side, but does the diner need to *see* "Table 12" reflected back at them?); (c) the "service staff has no access to review data" guarantee (2.1) should be made *visible to the diner*, not just true in the schema — e.g., a single line on the landing screen: "Staff never see individual responses." This is a 1-sentence, on-brand, trust-building addition that's currently entirely absent from the diner-facing copy spec (Section 4.2).

### HIGH

**1.4 — "Happy diners don't review" is named (12.2) but the mitigation is a documentation note, not a design change.**
The plan correctly identifies that all-positive self-selection bias is "inherent... not fixable by UX alone" and recommends framing the admin dashboard around *relative* comparison rather than absolute scores. But this is the **single most consequential bias in the entire system** — if the population of reviewers skews toward people with something to say (often something *negative*, but also disproportionately the *extremely* satisfied — the bimodal "extreme experience" pattern the plan cites for Google/TripAdvisor in G3) — and the plan's *only* mitigation is "bake this framing into dashboard copy." That's a caption, not a fix.
*Why it matters:* The product's core sales pitch is "more honest than Google Reviews" (G3) — but if the *same selection mechanism* (only people motivated enough to pull out their phone) drives both, the structural bias may be **more similar to Google Reviews than the plan admits**, just with better per-dish granularity. The plan never asks: *what % of diners who had a perfectly fine, unremarkable meal will scan a QR code at all?* — and unremarkable-but-fine is the experience an honest signal most needs to capture, because it's also the median experience.
*Fix:* Add a **lightweight, non-intrusive nudge during the meal** that's distinct from "ask happy tables" (which 3.5 correctly forbids) — e.g., the QR card itself, present at *every* table from the moment they sit down (not just at the end), with copy that frames participation as routine/expected rather than exceptional ("we ask everyone — takes about a minute"). This is a *floor plan / operations* change, not a software change, but it belongs in this plan because it's the single highest-leverage move against self-selection bias, and it costs nothing extra to build.

**1.5 — Per-table rate limit (20/hour) is in tension with the large-party scenario the plan itself raises elsewhere.**
Section 3.4 #4 sets "~20 reviews per table per hour" as "generous enough that a table of 12 people each submitting 1-2 reviews never hits it." Do the math: 12 people × up to 2 submissions (if the "supplemental note" path from already-reviewed also counts toward this limit — the plan doesn't clarify whether `review_type = 'supplemental'` rows count against the per-table cap) = up to 24, **already over the 20 cap**, before counting any review-per-dish granularity questions. More importantly: a table of 12 ordering family-style means **8-10+ shared dishes**, and if even half the table rates 3-4 shared dishes each... the `reviews` table cap (one row per session) isn't the bottleneck — but if the cap is *also* checked against `review_dish_ratings` inserts (the SQL function `submit_review` doesn't show the rate-limit check's exact scope), a large table doing thorough per-dish reviews could hit it.
*Why it matters:* If the rate limit fires for a *legitimate* large party mid-flow, the diner sees: *"Thanks for your enthusiasm — we've reached our review capacity for this table right now."* For the 8th person at a 12-top who's been diligently rating 4 dishes, being told to "try again later, or speak to a member of staff" after they've already invested 60+ seconds is a **severe, brand-damaging failure mode** at exactly the demographic (large celebratory parties) most likely to have strong, useful per-dish opinions about a family-style menu.
*Fix:* Clarify the cap's exact scope in the SQL (does it count `reviews` rows only, or also gate on `review_dish_ratings` volume?), and set it as a function of **typical table capacity**, not a flat number — e.g., per-table cap = `max(20, table_capacity × 3)`, pulled from `restaurant_tables.label`/a new `capacity` column. A flat 20 is calibrated for 2-4 tops and will misfire on exactly the parties most worth capturing.

**1.6 — Brigading defense explicitly accepts "slow/subtle brigading" as undetectable — but doesn't model the *adjacent* risk: a single bad-faith review from staff/competitor that's indistinguishable from a real one and gets featured.**
Section 3.7's trade-off (accepting slow brigading to avoid false-positive suppression of real negative trends) is *reasonable as a population-level statement*. But the dashboard surfaces **individual comments** ("Recent Comments," dish-detail "Free-text comments for this dish") to David. A single, well-written, plausible-sounding fake negative comment about a specific dish (from a competitor, an ex-employee, or even a personal grudge against a chef) is **statistically invisible** (it won't trigger burst detection, divergence flags, or low-effort flags if written carefully) but **individually visible and individually actionable** — David reads it, believes it, and might pull a dish from the menu over one fabricated comment.
*Why it matters:* The plan's integrity mechanisms operate at the *aggregate* level (burst detection, distribution shape), but the dashboard's most emotionally salient surface — individual free-text comments — has **zero integrity tooling** applied to it at the individual-comment level. One person's bad day, or one person's bad faith, reads identically to David.
*Fix:* No new suppression mechanism (that would violate 3.5's correct principles) — instead, **contextualize every individual comment with its dish's aggregate** right where it's displayed: "Miso Soup — ★2 + comment: '...' (this dish: ★3.0 avg from 11 reviews this week)". This costs nothing architecturally (the data already exists) and converts "one scary sentence" into "one data point among eleven," which is the correct epistemic frame and is currently absent from the `/admin/dishes/[id]` spec (Section 2.3).

### MEDIUM

**1.7 — The divergence flag (rating vs. sentiment mismatch) will fire constantly on a specific, predictable pattern that isn't "sarcasm" — it's the brand's own neutral-framing working as intended.**
Section 3.3's redesigned scale (*"Didn't work for me / Below expectations / As expected / Better than expected / Exceptional"*) deliberately makes "3 = As expected" feel **genuinely neutral**, not a failure state. But a lexicon-based sentiment classifier (3.6, MVP Option A) operating on free text will very likely classify "it was fine, nothing special" or "exactly what I expected" as **neutral-to-slightly-negative** (lexicon classifiers key on words like "fine," "nothing," "ok" which skew negative in most off-the-shelf VADER-style lexicons calibrated on product reviews, not on a brand that's *deliberately* made "as expected" a non-judgment). A 3★ rating + "exactly what I expected, good but not life-changing" comment could trigger `flagged_divergence` (rating=3 isn't in the `<=2` or `>=4` divergence bands, so *technically* this specific example won't fire — but the *mid-band* miscalibration is symptomatic of a broader issue: the lexicon was never validated against *this brand's specific scale semantics*).
*Why it matters:* If `/admin/integrity` shows a steady stream of "divergence" flags that are actually just "the sentiment classifier doesn't understand that 'as expected' is this brand's neutral," David either (a) starts ignoring the divergence flag entirely (dead feature), or (b) starts second-guessing genuinely useful divergence signals.
*Fix:* Phase 4's lexicon classifier needs a **brand-specific calibration pass** — even a small custom wordlist override (e.g., explicitly map "as expected," "fine," "good," "nice" toward neutral-to-positive given this brand's scale semantics) before shipping, not as a post-launch tune. This is a half-day task that should be in Phase 4's checklist and currently isn't.

**1.8 — "Comparison to public review platforms" (1.2) as a quarterly manual check has no owner, no process, and will not happen.**
Listed as a proxy signal with "manual quarterly check, not automated." Nothing in Section 11's phases assigns this to anyone, creates a template/checklist for it, or schedules it. Manual quarterly tasks with no owner and no calendar trigger have a near-100% failure rate in small businesses.
*Fix:* Either cut it from the proxy-signal table (if it's not going to happen, don't claim it as a mitigation), or make it concrete: a `/admin/settings` field "Last external review check: [date]" with a gentle reminder banner if >100 days, turning a vague intention into a trackable habit. Low effort, closes the gap between "named" and "done."

### LOW

**1.9 — `user_agent_hash` as a "coarse bot signal" will produce enormous false-positive collisions.**
The vast majority of diners at a restaurant in a single evening will be on a small handful of UA strings (iOS Safari on 2-3 recent iPhone models, Android Chrome on 2-3 common Android models) — SHA-256 hashing a UA string doesn't add entropy, it just obscures a value that was already low-entropy. Two unrelated diners on identical phone models will produce **identical** `user_agent_hash` values. If this is ever used to eyeball "same device resubmitting," it will flag unrelated people constantly.
*Fix:* Either drop this column (it adds privacy-review surface area for very little signal — 9.5 already acknowledges the residual risk), or be explicit in `/admin/integrity` copy that UA-hash matches are expected/noisy and not meaningful in isolation, only as one of several burst-detection inputs.

## LENS 2 — Diner UX / Friction

### CRITICAL

**2.1 — The locked "no POS in MVP" decision + the actual menu (16 categories, 183 items, heavy multi-variant pricing) makes the "empty state" (full menu) the *only* state — and it is not a <60s experience for dish selection alone.**
This is the plan's most consequential collision between a **locked architectural decision** (6.4: no order-context, full menu always shown) and the **actual content** it has to operate on (183 items across 16 categories, where "Mains" alone has 39 items, many differing only by protein: "Thai Red Curry" / "Thai Green Curry" / "Thai Panang Curry" / "Thai Massaman Curry" / "Japanese Curry" — five visually-similar curry names a diner must distinguish from memory, in a collapsed accordion, on a 375px screen, to find "the curry we had").
Walk the realistic flow for a 2-top who ordered 3 dishes:
1. Scan → land → tap Begin (~5-10s, generous)
2. `/select`: 16 collapsed category accordions. Diner doesn't remember which *category* "that fish thing" was in (Mains? Grills? Sashimi?). Expands 2-3 categories, scrolls, scans 39+25+18 item names looking for 3 specific dishes among visually-similar names (~30-60s, realistically — this is the part the plan's "search box" mitigates only if the diner remembers the *exact printed name*, which for "Crispy Fried Fish with Tamarind Fish" or "Asian Style Mushroom" is not guaranteed)
3. 3x per-dish rating screens (5-point scale + 4 tag chips + optional comment each) — even at a brisk 15-20s/dish = 45-60s
4. Overall screen (5-point + 5 chips + comment) — 15-20s
5. Submit + thank-you

**Total realistic median: 100-150 seconds** for a diner who actually engages with per-dish rating (the system's core value prop, G2) — not the <90s target, and *far* from "effortless." The plan's own success metric (1.2) sets median <90s as the bar and the *only* way to hit it is via the skip-to-overall path, which sacrifices G2 entirely.
*Why it matters:* This isn't a tuning problem, it's a structural one: **the locked decisions (no POS + full menu + multi-dish rating) are jointly incompatible with the locked target (<90s median)** given this menu's actual shape. The plan documents both halves of this contradiction (Section 12.2 even lists "low scan rate" and "diners rate only 1 dish" as risks) but never says the quiet part: *the contradiction is the design*.
*Fix (within the locked constraints):* (a) **Cap the realistic ask** — explicitly design for "1-2 dishes is the success case, not 3-4" in all copy and UX (the skip path already does this implicitly; make it explicit — e.g., after rating 2 dishes, offer "[Continue to next dish] / [That's enough — go to overall]" rather than forcing all selected dishes); (b) **Make `/select` a *recency-and-popularity-ranked* list, not a 16-category alphabetical-by-category accordion** — even without POS integration, a static "Frequently ordered" section pulled from historical `review_dish_ratings` volume (admin-curated or auto-computed) at the top of `/select` would dramatically cut search time for the ~20-30 dishes that represent 80% of actual orders, without needing live POS data. This is buildable in MVP from data the system will already have after week 1, and isn't mentioned anywhere.

**2.2 — Shared/family-style dishes have no design at all — and this menu is *built* for family-style ordering.**
Items like "Seafood Platter (Tuna, Salmon, Prawn) — 130,000," "Sashimi Platter (Small/Medium/Large)," "Nigiri Platter," and the entire "Sushi" sections read as **dishes meant to be shared across a table**, not individually plated. The diner journey (2.2) treats every selected dish identically — "How was the [dish name]?" — with no accommodation for "this was for the whole table, I only had 2 pieces" vs. "this was my individual main."
*Why it matters:* For a shared platter, (a) multiple people at the table may each select and rate the *same* platter, each rating it based on a different subset of what was on it (one person mostly ate the salmon nigiri, another the tuna sashimi) — producing N "ratings" of one shared object that don't represent N independent opinions of the same thing; (b) the per-dish tag chips ("portion," "value") are nearly meaningless for a shared platter rated by one person who had 1/8th of it.
*Fix:* No new screen needed — a single, optional micro-affordance on the per-dish rating screen for items the admin has flagged as `is_shareable` (a one-time tag on ~10-15 platter/sharing items in `menu_items`, added during seed): a small secondary line under the dish name, *"Shared dish — rate based on what you tried"* — this reframes the rating without adding a question, costs one boolean column + one seed-time tagging pass, and prevents the "portion" tag chip from reading as nonsensical on a platter.

### HIGH

**2.3 — "I didn't order that" / variant ambiguity isn't a hypothetical edge case — it's baked into ~40% of the menu's structure.**
Counting items with `/`-separated variant descriptions in the sample menu (Thai Red/Green/Panang/Massaman Curry, Pad Thai/Udon/Soba/Garlic Glass/Fried/Sukiyaki Noodles, Edamame, Cashewnut, Wantons, Satay, Tom Yum, Tom Kha, Sweet Corn Soup, Laab, Nam Tok, Veg Salad, Fried Rice variants, etc.) — a large fraction of the menu is printed as **one line, multiple proteins/sizes, multiple prices**. Section 12.2 names this as a "medium/medium" risk and defers resolution to "Phase 1 seed against real data" — but this is a **diner-facing UX question, not a seed-script question**. When a diner selects "Thai Green Curry" from `/select`, do they see *one* rating screen for "Thai Green Curry" (ambiguous — they had the Chicken variant at 45,000, someone else at the table had the Veg at 40,000 — are these "the same dish"?) — and does the per-dish "value" tag chip mean anything when the diner doesn't know if their rating screen is implicitly about their specific variant or the dish-as-a-concept?
*Why it matters:* This affects **dozens of menu items**, not a handful — it's not an edge case, it's close to the median case for this specific menu. Deferring it to "Phase 1 seed" means it'll be resolved by an engineer's seed-script convenience, not a diner-experience decision.
*Fix:* Resolve this *now*, in this plan, as a diner-UX decision (then let Phase 1 implement it): the per-dish rating screen headline should reflect the *general dish name* ("How was the Thai Green Curry?") since that's what's rated — but the screen should also show the printed variant line as context-setting subtext ("Veg · Chicken · Pork — rate it however you had it"), making explicit to the diner that variant-level distinction isn't expected. This is a one-sentence copy addition to Section 7's spec, costs nothing, and removes diner confusion that currently has no answer.

**2.4 — "Didn't finish the dish" / sent food back has no signal path — and it's one of the highest-value signals for a restaurant owner.**
The tag-chip vocabulary (Taste / Portion / Value / Presentation for dishes; Service / Ambiance / Speed / Value / Cleanliness for visit) has no way to express "this came out wrong and I sent it back" or "I couldn't finish it (too much / too rich / quality issue)." A diner who sent a dish back has, from a kitchen-ops perspective, **the single most actionable feedback possible** — and currently has only a 1-5 number and an optional free-text field to express it (and per 3.3, the comment field is optional and many people won't write paragraphs explaining what happened).
*Why it matters:* For G2 ("per-dish signal that's actionable"), "sent back / didn't finish" is *more* actionable than a 2★ rating with no context — a 2★ with "didn't finish — too salty" tells David exactly what to check with the kitchen; a bare 2★ tells him almost nothing.
*Fix:* Add **one** additional dish-level tag option to the existing 4 (Taste/Portion/Value/Presentation): something like "Didn't finish" or "Wasn't as described" — framed neutrally per 3.3's principles (not "Sent back — angry" but a calm factual toggle). This is a one-line addition to the fixed enum in `chk_dish_tags_valid` (5.2) and the chip set in 7.3 — minimal schema/UI cost, meaningfully closes a real gap in G2.

**2.5 — Battery, connectivity, and the *initial* network round-trip aren't addressed — only the *final* submit's network error is.**
Section 2.2's error-handling diagram only shows a retry path for the **final submit**. But per 6.2's "correction," the **first** network call (`POST /api/sessions` on landing) is what creates the session row and sets the cookie — this happens *before* the diner has invested any time, on a lakeside-restaurant connection that may be patchy. If this call fails or times out, what does the diner see? The plan's `/r/[tableCode]` "Loading" state (4.2) only describes the *table/menu resolution* loading state (target <500ms) — it's unclear whether `/api/sessions` is part of that same critical path or fire-and-forget, and what happens if it fails silently (does "already-reviewed" detection break for this diner on a re-scan, since no session row exists?).
*Fix:* Explicitly extend the error-state spec (4.2) to cover landing-page network failure for the session-bootstrap call, with the same calm-retry pattern already designed for final submit — currently this is an unstated gap, not a designed state.

### MEDIUM

**2.6 — The "skip to overall" framing ("No dishes selected — that's OK...") is good, but there's no equivalent mid-flow escape after rating 1 of N dishes.**
Once a diner has selected 3 dishes and started rating, the flow (per 2.2's diagram) is "repeat per dish" with `[Next dish →] / [Back]` — there's no "I'm done rating dishes, take me to overall" exit *after* dish 1 if the diner selected 3 but only wants to rate 1. This forces either (a) completing all 3 (more friction than they signed up for) or (b) abandoning entirely (zero data, per 6.2's all-or-nothing model — *losing the one dish they did rate*).
*Fix:* Every per-dish rating screen's progress indicator ("1 of 3") should include a low-emphasis "[Skip remaining →]" link alongside `[Next dish →]`, routing to `/overall` with whatever per-dish data has been entered so far preserved in the reducer. This directly serves 12.2's named risk ("diners rate only 1 dish then abandon") by converting "abandon = zero data" into "skip = partial data," which the reducer already supports per 6.5's `submit_review` function (it only inserts dish rows where *something* was provided) — this is a UI-only addition to an already-compatible backend.

**2.7 — "Already reviewed" soft wall + "leave a note" secondary path could become the *primary* path for a multi-course meal, and its framing doesn't account for that.**
A diner who reviews after appetizers, then re-scans after the main course (same 12-hour window), hits "already reviewed." The secondary "leave a note" path (4.2) is designed as an afterthought ("Something else on your mind?") for occasional supplementary feedback — but for a multi-course Japanese-fusion meal where appetizers/sushi/mains/dessert genuinely arrive in waves, **this could be the *expected* pattern for a meaningful fraction of diners**, and "supplemental notes not counted toward per-dish/overall numeric aggregates" means their *dessert* opinion (rated via the supplemental free-text-only path) never becomes structured per-dish data at all.
*Fix:* Not a CRITICAL fix, but worth flagging: if analytics (1.2) show high re-scan rates within the service window, the "supplemental" path should be revisited to support at least one more *structured* per-dish rating (not just free text) — currently the schema treats it as text-only, which under-serves what might be a common multi-wave dining pattern at this kind of restaurant.

### LOW

**2.8 — Haptic feedback (7.6 #6) on rating-selection could feel like a slot machine, not a brush stroke, on Android.**
"A tiny tactile confirmation... common in premium native apps" — true for iOS's refined haptic engine, but Android's `navigator.vibrate()` Web API produces a single blunt buzz with no equivalent to iOS's calibrated "tap" feel. On a majority-Android user base (likely, for this market), a vibration on every rating-circle tap risks feeling like a **notification/gamification cue** (closer to a slot-machine "ding" than a calligraphic brush stroke), which actively works against the "calm, restrained" brand. *Fix:* either iOS-only (feature-detect for the richer Haptics API specifically, not just `navigator.vibrate` presence), or test on real Android hardware before committing to this as a premium signal — it may need to be cut for Android.

## LENS 3 — Admin Usefulness

### CRITICAL

**3.1 — With 183 menu items and realistic review volumes, most "per-dish" data will be N=0-3/week — and the dashboard presents this as if it were signal.**
The wireframe (2.3) shows "NEEDS ATTENTION: ⚠ Thai Green Curry ★3.2 (6)" sitting visually equivalent to "TOP-RATED: Salmon Nigiri ★4.8 (12)." With 183 items and (per the plan's own 60% completion / per-session-dish-count uncertainty) probably **dozens of reviews per week total**, spread across some subset of 183 dishes, **the overwhelming majority of dishes will have 0-3 ratings in any given week**. A dish with **1 review at 2★** will mathematically show as "★2.0 (1)" — is that "needs attention"? A statistically literate person says "n=1, meaningless." The dashboard, as specified, has **no minimum-sample-size gate, no confidence indication, and no distinction between "★3.0 (11)" (real signal) and "★2.0 (1)" (noise)** in either the Top-Rated/Needs-Attention framing or the sortable `/admin/dishes` table.
*Why it matters:* This is the **single biggest risk to G5** ("operable without a dedicated data person") — a non-technical owner has no intuition for "n=1 is noise," and the dashboard's visual language (a star rating + a parenthetical count, both in the same small font size) doesn't *teach* that intuition. David could pull a dish from the menu because of one bad night from one diner, dressed up by the UI to look exactly as authoritative as a dish with 50 reviews.
*Fix:* This needs a **designed minimum-N threshold** baked into the "Top Rated"/"Needs Attention" lists specifically (not the full `/admin/dishes` table, which can show everything with the count visible) — e.g., these two curated lists only populate from dishes with **N ≥ 5** in the selected period, with a footnote: *"Dishes with fewer than 5 reviews this period aren't shown here — check `/admin/dishes` for everything, including newer/rarer items."* This is a single `HAVING count >= 5` clause in the existing `dish_rating_summary` view's consuming query — near-zero engineering cost, and it's the difference between "looks like $100k" (considered, doesn't oversell weak data) and "looks like a free WordPress plugin" (shows every number it has, regardless of whether the number means anything).

### HIGH

**3.2 — "Compare to dish-level menu average" — but the "menu average" itself shifts as more data arrives, and isn't a stable yardstick for a new dish.**
The framing (12.2: "this dish vs. menu average is the more reliable signal") implies a stable baseline. But the "menu average" is itself computed from the same self-selecting, all-positive-skewed population (12.2's own "All-positive bias" risk) — and for a **newly-added seasonal dish** (which `menu_items.is_active` explicitly supports toggling), there's no baseline at all for its first 1-2 weeks. More subtly: if the restaurant's overall quality genuinely improves over a quarter (kitchen hires a new chef), the "menu average" rises with it — a dish that was "above average" in Q1 might show as "below average" in Q2 *purely because the bar moved*, with the dashboard giving David no way to see that the *absolute* number for that dish didn't change, only its *relative* position did.
*Why it matters:* "Compare to average" as the *primary* framing (which 12.2 explicitly recommends baking into dashboard copy) can mislead exactly the kind of longitudinal "are we getting better" question David most cares about (1.2's "sentiment trend ↑↓→" KPI).
*Fix:* Show **both** framings side-by-side wherever a dish's rating appears: the dish's own trend-over-time (already in scope, dish-detail page) *and* its position relative to the current-period menu average — with the copy making explicit that these answer different questions ("Is this dish improving?" vs. "Is this dish keeping pace with everything else?"). Currently the plan picks one framing (relative) as primary without surfacing the other.

**3.3 — The `/admin/integrity` page is the plan's most-built feature for the audience least likely to use it as designed.**
Section 2.3 specifies a dedicated page with: submission-time histogram, "too fast" flag count, per-dish distribution-shape warnings (unimodal vs. bimodal), and burst-detection banners. This is **excellent thinking for a data scientist** and genuinely necessary for the product's integrity promise to be *true*. But persona "David" (2.1) is explicitly "not a data analyst" and checks the dashboard "a few times a week." A page full of histograms and statistical-shape warnings, with copy like *"Sentiment is a rough signal, not a verdict"* (12.2's own mitigation for a *different* problem) risks becoming **the page David never opens** — while still counting as a major chunk of Phase 4's build effort (4-5 checklist items).
*Why it matters:* If `/admin/integrity` is built exactly as specified but unused, two things happen: (a) the engineering investment doesn't pay off in the "operable by non-technical owner" goal (G5), and (b) — more importantly — **the burst-detection banner, which is the system's actual defense against brigading (3.7), only works if someone reads it**. An unread integrity page means the brigading defense is *theoretically* present but *operationally* absent.
*Fix:* The burst-detection banner (3.7) shouldn't live exclusively on a separate `/admin/integrity` page that requires a deliberate visit — it should be a **dashboard-level alert** (e.g., a dismissible banner at the top of `/admin/dashboard` itself, "Unusual activity detected...") that surfaces *into the page David actually opens*, with `/admin/integrity` remaining as the deep-dive destination for those who click through. This is a placement change, not new functionality, but it's the difference between a real defense and a theoretical one.

**3.4 — Nothing in the admin experience tells David *what to do* — only *what happened*.**
The dashboard surfaces ratings, trends, tags, and comments — all observational. The tag-chip frequency chart ("Taste / Portion / Value / Presentation" selected-counts per dish) is the closest thing to a "why," but it's a frequency count of *categories*, not a synthesis ("3 comments this week on Miso Soup mention 'cold'"). For G5 (non-technical owner, no dedicated data person), the gap between "Miso Soup is ★3.0, and 'Taste' was the most-selected tag" and "check with the kitchen about Miso Soup being served cold" is a gap David has to bridge himself, every time, across however many flagged dishes there are.
*Why it matters:* This is the difference between a *reporting* tool and a *decision-support* tool — and the plan's own G5 ("answer 'what should we fix this week?' without SQL") implies decision support, but the actual screens (2.3) are reporting.
*Fix:* This doesn't need an LLM-powered "insights" feature (over-engineering risk the plan rightly avoids elsewhere) — a simple, **rule-based weekly digest** would close most of the gap: a single auto-generated paragraph at the top of `/admin/dashboard`, computed server-side from the same data already shown ("This week: Salmon Nigiri remains your top dish (★4.8, 12 reviews). Miso Soup dropped to ★3.0 (11 reviews, down from ★3.6 last week) — 4 of the 11 comments mention temperature."). This is template-based text generation over existing aggregates + a basic keyword match on comments for the dish with the largest week-over-week drop — buildable in Phase 3/4 without new infrastructure, and it's the single highest-leverage addition for G5.

### MEDIUM

**3.5 — Export usage tracking (1.2) has no destination — what happens with the exported CSV?**
"Export usage — if nobody ever exports, the export feature is dead weight (fine, but track it)" is honest, but the plan doesn't ask the *next* question: for a restaurant owner with "no dedicated data person" (G5), what would they even *do* with a raw CSV of anonymized reviews? Pivot tables in Excel aren't a "David" activity per the persona (2.1). The export feature, as specified (date range + category filter + CSV), is built for a persona that doesn't exist in this plan (a data-literate user) — yet it's in MVP (Phase 4) while the digest-style synthesis (3.4 above) that *would* serve David isn't.
*Fix:* Not necessarily cut from MVP (it's cheap and "raw data available" is a good integrity signal per 3.5's principles) — but don't count its absence-of-use as neutral. If usage is near-zero after 60 days, that's a signal to *reallocate* that effort toward decision-support framing (3.4), and the plan should say so explicitly as a post-launch checkpoint rather than leaving export as a permanent, unexamined fixture.

**3.6 — The trend chart's "review volume" secondary axis could itself become a vanity metric without context.**
"TREND CHART — overall visit rating, last 30 days (line) + review volume (bar, secondary axis)" (2.3). Raw review *volume* going up could mean: more diners, better QR placement, a promotion, or — per 3.7 — a brigade. Shown as a bare bar chart with no annotation, a spike in volume reads as "more engagement = good" by default chart-reading conventions (bigger bars = better), which is exactly backwards if the spike correlates with a rating *drop* (which 3.7's burst-detection logic would catch — but only on `/admin/integrity`, not on this chart, per 3.3 above).
*Fix:* If 3.3's fix (surface burst-detection on the main dashboard) ships, this becomes low-risk automatically — flagging it here because it's another instance of the same root cause (integrity signals live on a page David doesn't visit).

## LENS 4 — Brand Fidelity & $100k Feel

### CRITICAL

**4.1 — `--color-border-strong` (Stone-500, #A89E86), the token the plan relies on for "non-color indicators of state," measures 2.31:1 against Washi-100 — below the 3:1 WCAG 1.4.11 minimum for UI-component/state-indicator contrast.**
Section 10's accessibility table makes a specific, falsifiable claim: *"Selected rating position: filled + lifted shadow + label text change (not color alone). Selected chips: filled background + border-weight change... (not color alone)."* The **unselected** states for both the rating circles (7.2: "outlined in `--color-border-strong`") and chips (7.3: "`--color-border-strong` outline") rely on this token being *visible enough to read as a UI element at all* — its outline is the only thing distinguishing "an empty tappable circle" from "blank space" before any interaction. At **2.31:1** against the washi background, this fails even the lenient 3:1 non-text-contrast bar (let alone looking "crisp" against a deliberately low-contrast, calm palette).
I separately checked `--color-focus` (Brass-600, #A1762C) against Washi-100: **3.55:1** — passes 3:1, but with almost no headroom, and the plan's own "focus styles never removed" rule (10) means this thin gold ring is the *only* focus indicator on a brand that's intentionally avoiding harsh contrast everywhere else.
*Why it matters:* This isn't a hypothetical accessibility nitpick — it's a **direct contradiction between a stated design token's measured properties and a stated accessibility requirement**, both written by the same plan. If shipped as specified, either (a) the unselected rating circles/chips will look like they have *no border at all* on many displays (reading as "broken" / "not designed," the opposite of $100k), or (b) implementation will quietly diverge from the token (someone darkens the border in code), violating 12.3's "every color is a token, zero hardcoded hex" checklist item — the plan would fail its own QA gate either way.
*Fix:* This needs to be resolved **in the token file**, not left to component-level guesswork: either introduce a new token (e.g., `--color-border-interactive`, darker than `--color-border-strong`, calibrated to ≥3:1) specifically for unselected-but-tappable element outlines, or accept that unselected rating circles/chips need a **second non-color cue at rest** (not just on selection) — e.g., a very subtle `--shadow-sm` on every circle/chip, selected or not, so the *shape* reads via shadow even where the border contrast is marginal. Either fix is small, but it must happen at the design-system level before Phase 2, because dozens of components depend on it.

### HIGH

**4.2 — The admin dashboard wireframe (Section 2.3) contains a literal ⚠️ emoji — directly violating the brand voice guidelines this same plan cites.**
Line-for-line, the plan's own ASCII wireframe reads: `⚠ Spicy Fried Hamour ★3.1 (8)` / `⚠ Miso Soup ★3.0 (11)` / `⚠ Thai Green Curry ★3.2 (6)`. The brand guidelines (read directly for this review) state under Voice & Tone: **"Don't: hype, exclamation spam, emoji clutter."** A warning emoji on a "Needs Attention" list is the single most generic, default-SaaS-dashboard visual idiom there is — it's the *opposite* of "editorial report, not a SaaS template" (7.6 #8).
*Why it matters:* This is small in isolation, but it's **diagnostic**: it shows that even the team that wrote 1,279 lines of careful brand-restraint reasoning (Section 7) reached, by *default*, for a warning-triangle emoji the moment they sketched a real screen with real "bad news" data on it. That's exactly the moment the brand is most tested — when something needs to look *urgent* without looking *alarmist* — and the wireframe's instinct was the generic one. If this instinct shipped into actual component code, "Needs Attention ⚠" would sit directly next to "Top Rated 🏆"-style framing that turns calm per-dish data into a **scoreboard**, which contradicts 3.5's entire point about not editorializing the data with red/green/alarm visual language (7.5 explicitly bans red/green for exactly this reason — but the wireframe's ⚠ achieves the same editorializing effect via emoji instead of color).
*Fix:* Replace `⚠` with the brand's own vocabulary — an eyebrow label is already specified elsewhere ("NEEDS ATTENTION" in `--ws-eyebrow` uppercase tracking-wide, no icon needed — the *label itself* says what's needed, calmly). Section 7.5's matcha/clay dual-tone histogram approach (calm, non-alarming) should extend to this list too: no icon at all, just the eyebrow label and the data. This is a one-line wireframe fix, but it should be explicitly called out as a "don't do this in the real build" annotation, because wireframes have a way of becoming implementation defaults.

**4.3 — The rating control's "dynamic label below the circles" (7.2) risks exactly the layout shift that 12.3's QA checklist explicitly bans — and the spec doesn't address it.**
7.2 specifies: tapping a position shows behavioral label text for that position ("Below expectations," "As expected," "Exceptional," etc. — 3.3's wording). These labels have **substantially different character counts** ("As expected" = 11 chars vs. "Better than expected" = 20 chars vs. "Didn't work for me" = 18 chars). If the label container isn't given a fixed height/width *and* the labels aren't center-aligned within a reserved space, **tapping different rating positions will cause the label area to reflow**, and on a narrow viewport (375px floor target, 4.4), a wider label could even wrap to a second line where a shorter one didn't — shifting everything below it (the tag chips, the comment field, the sticky action bar's visual relationship to content above it).
12.3's own "Diner experience" checklist item: *"Every transition feels intentional — no jarring layout shifts."* This is the **most interacted-with element in the entire diner flow** (every dish, every visit, the diner taps this and watches the label change) — if it shifts, it shifts on every single rating, every time, for every diner.
*Why it matters:* Layout-shift on the highest-frequency interaction is the kind of thing that *individually* seems minor but *cumulatively* is one of the most reliable ways a product feels "almost right" rather than "considered" — exactly the gap between "generic SaaS in brand colors" and "$100k."
*Fix:* Reserve a fixed-height (and ideally fixed-width, center-text) container for the label, sized to the *longest* label in the set at the target font size, determined once during Phase 5's implementation and added explicitly to the component spec — currently this is a one-paragraph gap in an otherwise extremely detailed UI spec (7.2), and it's the kind of gap that's invisible in a static mockup and only appears once someone actually taps through the real control.

**4.4 — 11 Higgsfield-generated "macro texture" images, used precisely *because* they should look hand-made/imperfect, carry a real risk of looking AI-generated/imperfect-in-the-wrong-way — and there's no review/rejection step in the plan.**
The prompts (Section 8) are well-written and specific. But macro/close-up textures (washi paper grain, ceramic glaze, linen weave, "barely-visible seigaiha pattern in the texture") are **exactly the category of image where current diffusion models most often produce subtly-wrong results** — repeating patterns that don't quite repeat, fiber/grain textures with an uncanny "too-perfect-then-glitchy" quality, or a "ripple pattern barely visible in the texture" (asset #1) that comes out looking like a compression artifact rather than a deliberate motif. For a brand whose **entire premise is "beauty in the imperfect... made by a human hand"** (brand guidelines, the literal "test for any decision"), an AI-generated texture that reads as "imperfect in an uncanny, machine way" rather than "imperfect in a human, hand-made way" would be a **direct, ironic violation of the brand's core philosophy** — and it's the kind of thing that's hard to articulate ("something feels off about this background") but cumulatively reads as "cheap."
*Why it matters:* Phase 5's checklist says "review against brand guidelines before integrating" (one line) — but "review against brand guidelines" for a *texture* requires a specific question ("does this look hand-made or does it look like a Midjourney wallpaper?") that the plan doesn't articulate, and there's no fallback plan if a generated asset fails this test (regenerate with adjusted prompt? source real photography instead? Section 6's "imagery & art direction" notes real photography is still pending/🟡).
*Fix:* Add an explicit acceptance criterion to Phase 5 for each of the 11 assets: a one-line "imperfection test" — *does this look like it was touched by a hand, or generated by a model?* — applied by a human reviewer, with a documented fallback (re-prompt with more specific imperfection cues — e.g., "uneven brush pressure," "visible tool marks," "asymmetric wear" — or substitute a real macro photo if 2-3 regeneration attempts all fail the test for a given asset). This is process, not a content change, but it's the difference between assets that *support* the brand promise and assets that *contradict* it while technically matching the prompt.

### MEDIUM

**4.5 — The "ensō stroke completing" loading animation (asset #3 + 7.6 #7) requires a vector path, but Higgsfield generates raster images — the pipeline has a gap.**
Asset #3's deliverable is specified as "800×800 (square, transparent PNG/SVG-traced)" and 7.6 #7 says it's "animated in CSS/SVG (stroke-dashoffset)". `stroke-dashoffset` is an SVG **path** animation technique — it requires a vector path with defined start/end points along the ensō's circumference, not a raster image (even a transparent PNG). "SVG-traced" implies a vectorization step (raster-to-vector tracing of the generated brushstroke) that **isn't a Higgsfield capability** and isn't listed as a task anywhere in Section 11's phases — Phase 5 says "Generate all 11 Higgsfield assets... Implement the rating control's brush-stroke fill + ensō loading animation" as if generation directly produces the animatable asset.
*Why it matters:* 7.6 #7 ("No loading spinners, ever... ensō stroke completing") is called out as a *signature* premium touch (it's #7 on the explicit "what makes it feel like $100k" list) — if the vectorization gap isn't caught until Phase 5 implementation, the team either (a) scrambles to hand-trace/recreate the asset as an SVG (extra unplanned work), or (b) falls back to a generic CSS spinner for this *specific* moment, which is exactly the "no loading spinners, ever" promise breaking at its highest-visibility instance (the submit button, used by every single diner).
*Fix:* Either (a) explicitly scope a vector-tracing step (manual, in a tool like Illustrator/Figma, from the Higgsfield still as reference — not automated) as its own Phase 5 task, or (b) decouple the visual reference from the implementation: have a designer/illustrator create the actual SVG path directly (using the Higgsfield still purely as an art-direction reference for the brushstroke's character), rather than expecting the generated asset to *be* the production artifact. Either is fine — but as written, Phase 5 has an implicit dependency that doesn't exist yet.

**4.6 — Recharts (7.5) is "themeable via the token palette" — but Recharts' default interaction affordances (tooltips, legends, axis lines) carry strong default-Material/default-web-chart visual conventions that fighting requires *more* customization effort than the plan's scope suggests.**
The plan correctly identifies the *visual* targets (brass line, matcha/clay histogram bars, no harsh gridlines) — these are achievable via Recharts' theming props. But "premium editorial chart" vs. "themed default chart library" is also about **hover tooltips** (Recharts' default tooltip is a white box with a drop shadow — exactly the "generic SaaS chrome" 7.6 #8 disclaims), **legend styling**, **axis tick formatting**, and **responsive breakpoint behavior** — none of which are mentioned in 7.5, and all of which are *highly visible* the moment a user (admin) actually interacts with a chart (hovers over a data point).
*Why it matters:* Charts are one of the few places in this product where a user *directly interacts* with a third-party UI library's defaults — every other surface (forms, buttons, cards) is custom-built per the token system. If chart *interactions* (not just static appearance) aren't included in the "$100k checklist" (12.3 doesn't mention tooltip/legend styling specifically), this is the most likely place for "looks custom at rest, looks like a Recharts demo on hover" to slip through.
*Fix:* Add explicit tooltip/legend/axis styling to Section 7.5's spec (custom tooltip component using `--color-surface`/`--shadow-md`/`--font-sans` rather than Recharts defaults) and to 12.3's brand-fidelity checklist ("chart *interactions* — not just static renders — use brand tokens, zero default-library chrome visible").

### LOW

**4.7 — "Sumi Night" dark theme exists in tokens but has zero presence anywhere in this plan's screens, phases, or QA checklist.**
`tokens.css` includes a complete dark-theme token override (`[data-theme="night"]`), and the brand guidelines (3) mention it ("A 'Sumi Night' dark theme is included in the tokens"). This plan never mentions it again — no screen uses it, no phase implements a theme toggle, and 12.3's checklist doesn't test it. Not a problem if it's *intentionally* out of scope for this system — but as written, it's simply **absent**, which for a "every token is used with meaning" brand (7.6 #3) leaves a fully-specified token set with no consumer. *Fix:* Either explicitly scope it out (one sentence: "Sumi Night is a brand-system asset for the main website, not used in the review system in MVP") or, if there's a plausible use (e.g., an evening-service diner flow that switches to dark automatically based on time-of-day — thematically apt for "lakeside, golden-hour to evening" per the brand's imagery direction, 6) flag it as a real post-MVP enhancement in Section 11's table. Currently it's neither — just unaddressed.

## Must Fix Before Build

These are the findings that, left unresolved, will either (a) cause the build to violate its own stated requirements/checklists, or (b) undermine the product's core promise in ways that are hard to retrofit later:

1. **(4.1)** Resolve the `--color-border-strong` / focus-ring contrast failure **in the token system** before Phase 2 — dozens of components depend on it, and as specified it contradicts the plan's own AA claims.
2. **(3.1)** Add a minimum-N gate (e.g., N≥5) to the "Top Rated"/"Needs Attention" curated lists — without it, the dashboard presents statistical noise as signal to a non-technical owner, which is the core G5 risk.
3. **(1.2)** Resolve the "exclude low-effort from headline average **by default**" design — either invert the default (show raw, annotate fast-reviews separately) or add an audit log for threshold changes. As specified, this is a curation lever the plan's own Section 3.5 principles would reject if applied to any other mechanism.
4. **(2.1)** Explicitly reconcile the <90s target with the 16-category/183-item full-menu reality — at minimum, ship a "frequently reviewed dishes" surfaced section on `/select` (buildable from data the system generates itself, no POS needed) and make "1-2 dishes rated" the explicit success framing in all copy, not an implicit fallback.
5. **(1.3)** Add diner-facing copy that addresses *perceived* (not just architectural) anonymity — table-number visibility, staff-access reassurance — currently the entire anonymity model is invisible to the person it's meant to reassure.
6. **(2.3)** Resolve the variant-ambiguity question (Thai Green Curry: Veg/Chicken/Pork as one rateable entity) as a diner-facing copy decision now, not a Phase 1 seed-script afterthought — it affects a large fraction of this specific menu.
7. **(4.2)** Strike the ⚠ emoji from the admin wireframe and replace with the brand's existing eyebrow-label vocabulary — small, but diagnostic of where default-SaaS instincts will surface unless explicitly guarded against.
8. **(4.4)** Add an explicit "hand-made vs. AI-generated" acceptance test + fallback plan to Phase 5 for the 11 Higgsfield textures — for *this specific brand*, a texture that reads as uncanny-AI is a direct philosophical contradiction, not just an aesthetic miss.

---

## Verdict

**Revise-first.** The architecture, integrity taxonomy, and brand-token system are unusually rigorous — this is not a shallow plan. But it has three *structural* tensions (locked decisions vs. locked targets vs. actual menu content) and at least one *direct self-contradiction* (accessibility claims vs. token contrast values) that should be resolved on paper before Phase 1, because each would otherwise surface mid-build as "wait, these two things in our own plan don't fit together" — at which point the fix is more expensive and more likely to be a rushed compromise rather than a considered one.
